CUSTOMER STORY / BABESIDE

The whole company on one AI workspace

Babeside was spending more than $5,000 a month on AI, yet the analysis worth acting on still depended on one person. This is the story of how a 50-person collectibles brand brought its channels, questions, and operating knowledge into one shared place.

Babeside sells hyper-realistic reborn baby dolls to families in more than 40 countries. The customer story describes a business that generated $47 million in the prior year, served more than one million families, and operated across a Shoplazza storefront and Amazon with a team of roughly 50 people.

From the outside, the brand already had scale: more than four billion video views, over 250,000 followers, a reported average order value of $155.85, and 85% of orders coming from new customers. Inside the company, however, the information needed to run that growth sat in different systems, different subscriptions, and one operator’s head.

01AT A GLANCE

The company, the moment before the change

Babeside’s growth created a familiar operating problem. The storefront could show one version of demand, Amazon another, and marketing platforms a third. None of those systems carried the whole story by itself. A channel dashboard could report what happened inside that channel, but it could not explain the business across DTC and Amazon in the language the team actually used.

That gap mattered because the company was not short on questions. Leaders wanted to know what moved last week, why Amazon traffic changed, which SPU carried DTC margin, and how the two channels were pacing together. Answering those questions required joining data, definitions, and history before the analysis could even begin.

02THE PROBLEM

$5K+ a month, and one power user

By early 2026, roughly 50 people at Babeside were using ChatGPT. The combined bill had climbed above $5,000 a month and was still rising. More tools were being proposed, but adding another subscription did not solve the underlying problem: the AI did not have the order history, margin definitions, or channel context needed to answer a business question responsibly.

Ask a generic model to project revenue without attaching order history or margins, and it can still produce a polished number. The problem is not that the answer sounds uncertain. It is that the answer can sound certain while the evidence is absent. Babeside was paying for access to AI, but not yet for a shared, inspectable understanding of its own business.

The useful analysis was concentrated elsewhere. According to the customer story, about 60% of the analysis worth acting on came from one Amazon operator. That person knew which number mattered, how Babeside defined it, and which caveat changed the answer. Everyone else could type into the same AI product, but they could not reproduce the same judgment.

The bottleneck was not access to a chatbot. It was access to the company’s data, definitions, and accumulated judgment.

03THE SHIFT

One brain, now everyone’s

Pulse brought Shoplazza, Amazon, Meta, and Google information into one shared system with 24 months of business history. The important change was not simply that four feeds appeared on one screen. It was that questions could now be answered against the same historical context while keeping the underlying sources visible.

The case study describes a different shape of answer. A projection came back with the calculation method, the source systems, and a confidence level. When the available data could not support a conclusion, the system was expected to surface the gap rather than fill it with a guess. That made uncertainty part of the answer instead of something hidden behind it.

The larger change happened in the open. The operator’s logic had never been fully written down; it lived in repeated questions, corrections, and habits. As the team worked through those questions together, definitions and decision patterns became reusable. New hires could begin with an existing operating context instead of reconstructing it from scratch, and knowledge did not have to leave when one person did.

04WHAT THE FOUNDER SEES

One place for the team, the spend, and the answers

Before the change, founder Rave Huang could see the AI bill rising but could not see what it was buying. The case study’s reconstructed workspace shows a different management view: one plan, member-level usage, remaining credits, and the team’s questions in one place. The goal was not surveillance of individual prompts; it was visibility into whether a company-wide capability was actually being used and where it was creating reusable value.

The spend became simpler as well. The customer story reports that more than $5,000 a month of scattered AI spend became a single $600 monthly line. Weekly and monthly readouts no longer had to be restarted from a blank page, and about 20 hours a week of reporting and rework were returned to the team. In the case study’s framing, that is roughly 1,000 hours over a year.

This did not remove the need for judgment. The company still had to confirm definitions, review evidence, and decide what to do. What changed was the starting point: instead of waiting for someone to pull data and rebuild the context, the team could begin with a prepared readout and spend its time on the decision.

06WHAT COMPOUNDS NEXT

From answers to assets

The story looks beyond the first operating change. It describes a path from individual answers to assets the company can keep: shared semantics, named events, decision boundaries, and reusable skills. In that model, “new customer,” “margin,” or “hero SPU” is defined once; a signal is named; a threshold explains when it matters; and a repeatable analysis describes what to review next.

The distinction between draft and decision remains important. The case study says AI can draft these definitions and routines, but a person confirms them. The operating instinct that once lived in one person’s head becomes explicit, reviewable, and available to others without pretending that the system should make the final business decision.

At the time of the story, scheduled Agent tasks were described as shipping that week, Freshdesk integration as in progress, and the broader semantics-to-skills model as newly planned. Those labels preserve the historical context of the customer journey; they are not an evergreen promise about today’s release status.

The real change was where the company started its questions

Babeside did not stop needing analysis. It stopped rebuilding the conditions for analysis every time someone asked a question. The company moved from scattered tools and private context toward one place where evidence, definitions, and uncertainty could travel with the answer.

That is why the headline result is bigger than a lower software bill. The cost became predictable, but the more durable outcome was organizational: more people could begin from the same business context, and the founder could see the company without waiting for the story to be assembled again.

Data scope, reported outcomes, and automation boundaries.

Which data sources were part of the Babeside story?

The story names Shoplazza, Amazon, Meta, Google, GA4, and Google Ads in different parts of the workflow, and describes four connected feeds with 24 months of history. Actual coverage depends on authorized accounts and the history available from each source.

Does Hologrow automatically change Babeside’s ads or store?

No. The workflow described here prepares analysis, monitoring, and recurring briefs for human review. It does not imply automatic changes to ads, prices, listings, inventory, orders, claims, payments, or customer records.

Are the savings and time results guaranteed?

No. The figures are specific to Babeside’s reported starting point, team, usage, and configuration. They are not a benchmark or a promise of results for another company.

Were the figures independently audited?

The customer story does not state that the figures were independently audited. This article therefore presents them as customer-specific, customer-reported evidence.
